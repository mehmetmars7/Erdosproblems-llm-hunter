\section*{\texorpdfstring{The Unique Games Conjecture}{The Unique Games Conjecture}}\label{43}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
For $[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}$, a unique game has a nonempty finite edge set and a permutation $\pi_e$ of $[k]$ on each directed edge $e=(v,w)$. Its optimum is
\[
 \operatorname{val}(G)=\max_{L:V\to[k]}\frac{\#\{e=(v,w):\pi_e(L(v))=L(w)\}}{|E|}.
\]
For every fixed $\varepsilon,\delta>0$ with $\varepsilon+\delta<1$, some fixed $k$ makes distinguishing $\operatorname{val}(G)\ge1-\varepsilon$ from $\operatorname{val}(G)\le\delta$ NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions. Intermediate values lie outside the promise. The alphabet size depends on the accuracy parameters, not on the growing input graph.
\par\smallskip\textit{Formulation and concept references:} \sref{43}{1}.
\cataloguescope{For every fixed pair of real constants epsilon, delta > 0 with epsilon + delta < 1, there exists an integer k >= 2, depending only on epsilon and delta, such that the following promise problem is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions. An instance is a finite directed constraint graph G=(V,E) with E nonempty and, for each edge e=(v,w), a permutation pi\_e of [k]. For a labeling L:V->[k], an edge is satisfied if pi\_e(L(v))=L(w); val(G) is the largest fraction of satisfied edges over all labelings. Distinguish val(G) >= 1-epsilon from val(G) <= delta, with no requirement on instances between these thresholds. The graph is the growing input; epsilon, delta and k are fixed constants. NP-hardness means that every language in NP has such a reduction mapping yes inputs to the first promised set and no inputs to the second.}
\subsection{Short English statement}
Is it computationally intractable to distinguish almost satisfiable permutation constraints from ones where almost none can be satisfied?

\subsection{Research attempt}

\paragraph{Current frontier.}
Khot's survey distinguishes exact satisfiability, which is efficiently
checkable, from the almost-satisfiable versus low-value promise in this
entry. Its Conjecture~2.5 has the required order of quantifiers.
\sref{43}{1}, Section~2.4. The 2025 journal version of
Dinur--Khot--Kindler--Minzer--Safra is an installment in the work leading
to the 2-to-2 Games Theorem, not a proof of the selected Unique Games
Conjecture. Its Corollary~1.12 has completeness $1/2$, conditional there
on its stated agreement hypothesis, rather than completeness arbitrarily
close to one. The work and its role in the subsequent chain are described
in the article's abstract and introduction. \sref{43}{2}, \eref{43}{1}.
The 2026 MAX-3-CUT result explicitly assumes UGC; it does not prove it.
\sref{43}{5}. No theorem is inferred from the title of the catalogue's
unreviewed vertex-cover proposal.

\paragraph{Attack route.}
I try to exploit the rigidity of permutations in both directions. For an
algorithm, propagate a root label and isolate the edges that obstruct
consistent propagation. The missing step is finding a small deletion set
without enumerating exponentially many possibilities. For hardness, try
to raise completeness from an already hard lower-completeness gap, using
subdivision or walk-composition gadgets. The missing step is preserving
low soundness while raising completeness; exact calculations below show
that these two elementary gadgets do not do it. This is an attempt to
break proposed approaches as well as to prove useful intermediate facts.

\paragraph{Attempt.}
Let $M=|E|>0$. When traversing an edge backwards, use the inverse
permutation. Take a spanning tree of each connected component of the
underlying undirected multigraph, ignoring loops for the tree. Choose a
root and let $P_v$ be the permutation obtained by propagation along its
unique tree path to $v$, with $P_{\rm root}$ the identity.

\textbf{Lemma 29.1 (a root-fixed-point formulation).}
All constraints in the component are simultaneously satisfiable if and
only if
\begin{equation}\label{r29:holonomy}
 \bigcap_{e=(v,w)}\operatorname{Fix}(P_w^{-1}\pi_eP_v)
                       \ne\varnothing.
\end{equation}
Moreover this can be checked, and a labeling produced when possible, in
$O(k(|V|+|E|))$ elementary permutation-table operations over all components.

\emph{Proof.} Any labeling satisfying tree edges is forced, once its root
label $a$ is given: $L(v)=P_v(a)$. The constraint on $e$ is exactly
$\pi_eP_v(a)=P_w(a)$, which is the fixed-point condition displayed.
Conversely any $a$ in the intersection gives a labeling satisfying every
edge. Each $P_v$ and each fixed-point condition is computed on a table
of $k$ labels. Isolated vertices are harmless, and loops are tested by
the same formula.\hfill$\square$

This reproves the basic propagation observation in \sref{43}{1}, and
makes explicit the cycle obstruction used next. It must not be confused
with a solution for $\operatorname{val}\ge1-\varepsilon$.

\textbf{Lemma 29.2 (exact dependence on a deletion budget).}
There is a labeling violating at most $b$ edges if and only if deleting
at most $b$ edges leaves a perfectly satisfiable constraint system.
Thus enumerating subsets of at most $b$ edges and applying
Lemma~29.1 decides this question in time
\begin{equation}\label{r29:deletion}
       O\!\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\min(b,M)}\binom Mj\,k(|V|+M)\right).
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof.} Delete the violated edges of a proposed labeling in one
direction. In the other, any labeling of the undeleted system violates
at most the deleted edges. This proves both the equivalence and the
finite enumeration bound.\hfill$\square$

For fixed $b$ this is polynomial. In the target $b$ may be
$\lfloor\varepsilon M\rfloor$ for a fixed positive $\varepsilon$, which
is linear in the growing instance size. Formula~\eqref{r29:deletion}
then supplies no polynomial algorithm. Replacing a small \emph{fraction}
by a small \emph{number} would silently weaken the problem.

Nor does checking only short cycles reveal every obstruction. On a
directed cycle of length $L$, put the identity permutation on $L-1$
edges and a fixed-point-free permutation on the last edge. The full
system is inconsistent, but deleting any edge makes it consistent;
its optimum is exactly $1-1/L$. Every proper path passes propagation.
For fixed $k\ge2$, a cyclic permutation of $[k]$ supplies the required
fixed-point-free map. This is an explicit near-satisfiable instance, not
an NP-hardness construction.

\medskip
\textbf{Attempt to upgrade completeness by subdivision.}
Replace each edge $e=(v,w)$ by a path of $\ell\ge1$ edges with fresh
internal vertices, choosing permutations on the path whose composition
is $\pi_e$. Denote the result by $G^{(\ell)}$.

\textbf{Lemma 29.3 (subdivision contracts both sides of the gap).}
\begin{equation}\label{r29:subdivision}
 \operatorname{val}(G^{(\ell)})
          =1-\frac{1-\operatorname{val}(G)}{\ell}.
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof.} For fixed endpoint labels, if the original edge is satisfied,
propagating through the new path satisfies all its edges. Otherwise at
least one new edge must fail, because satisfying them all would impose
the original endpoint relation. Exactly one failure is achievable by
propagating until the last edge. The internal vertices of different paths
are disjoint, so these optimal choices can be made independently for
fixed original labels. Minimize the total number of failed original
relations and divide by the new edge count $\ell M$.\hfill$\square$

Suppose a base reduction gives completeness $c$ and soundness $s<c$.
Subdivision changes its guaranteed thresholds to
$1-(1-c)/\ell$ and $1-(1-s)/\ell$; their difference is $(c-s)/\ell$.
It does raise completeness, but it also raises low soundness towards one.
For $c=1/2$, attaining $1-\varepsilon$ by this gadget requires
$\ell\ge1/(2\varepsilon)$, whereas even a value-zero instance becomes
$1-1/\ell$ satisfiable. Thus this gadget cannot turn a $1/2$-completeness
hardness theorem into the target small-soundness theorem. The equality
in \eqref{r29:subdivision}, rather than a loose analysis of the gadget,
is the obstruction.

\medskip
\textbf{Attempt to amplify inconsistency by composing walks.}
A second proposal puts a new constraint between the endpoints of each
length-$r$ walk, using the composed permutation. This still makes each
new constraint unique. But it tests only endpoint consistency, not the
individual constraints along the walk, and failed constraints can cancel.

Take the directed three-cycle and $k=2$, with the transposition on every
edge. Its optimum is $2/3$: alternating labels cannot close consistently
around an odd cycle, and one failed edge is achievable. Composing two
successive constraints gives the identity on each of the three new edges.
The all-equal labeling satisfies the entire resulting game, whose value
is one. This counterexample applies to the precise length-two composition
rule even though every constituent constraint is a permutation.
It disproves any assertion that this rule must preserve or increase the
unsatisfied fraction. It does not refute sophisticated gap amplification
using additional tests or a different alphabet.

Combining the two elementary gadgets therefore has no supplied
soundness proof. Subdivision has an exact adverse trade-off; endpoint
composition can erase all frustration. Retaining all intermediate labels
in a longer alphabet would allow one to test more information, but then
a two-endpoint relation must still be a permutation on the same fixed
alphabet and its completeness/soundness must be proved. That is a new
construction problem, not a consequence of storing a walk.

\paragraph{Stress test.}
All algorithms and gadgets above use the input graph as the growing
parameter. No value of $k$ depending on $|V|$ is substituted into the
conjecture. Checking a small alphabet, exact satisfiability, or a bounded
number of errors does not handle every fixed $(\varepsilon,\delta)$
with its possibly much larger fixed alphabet. An exponential exhaustive
search over the labels remains a finite algorithm, but is irrelevant to
a polynomial-time reduction or decision claim.

A polynomial-time solver for all these gaps would imply $\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}$
\emph{if} the asserted NP-hardness reductions exist; it would not, without
an additional separation assumption, logically disprove NP-hardness.
Conversely NP-hardness of a promise problem alone is not an unconditional
proof that no polynomial-time algorithm exists. These distinctions matter
because the selected target is an NP-hardness statement, not merely an
informal assertion of intractability. \sref{43}{1}, Sections~2.1 and~2.4.

The companion script enumerates small two-label directed-cycle games,
compares propagation with brute-force exact satisfiability, and checks
\eqref{r29:subdivision} for small subdivisions and the triangle
composition counterexample. All are exact finite checks; none tests the
asymptotic NP-hardness quantifier. The reference's allowance of more
general constraint graphs does not invalidate these counterexamples,
since these particular loop-free directed graphs already lie in scope.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Outcome.}
\textbf{Outcome: Exploratory approach with unresolved gap.}

The notebook obtains an explicit consistency/deletion formulation, an
exact subdivision trade-off, and a three-vertex obstruction to naive walk
amplification. The basic propagation algorithm is already known; no
historical novelty is asserted for these elementary deductions. They
identify why two routes do not reach almost-perfect completeness together
with arbitrarily low soundness. No reduction with the target parameters,
and no unconditional refutation of its NP-hardness, has been established.
