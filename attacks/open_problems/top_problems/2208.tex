\section{Near-Linear Numbers of Distances from One Point}\label{2208}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 2208 · Number Theory}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



Distances are Euclidean. Distinct distances mean distinct numerical values; when a distance is said to occur a certain number of times, unordered pairs of distinct points are counted. Congruence allows any Euclidean isometry, including reflection. \sref{2208}{1} \sref{2208}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Given $n$ distinct points $A\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ must there be a point $x\in A$ such that $ \#\{ d(x,y) : y \in A\} \gg n^{1-o(1)}? $ Or even $\gg n/\sqrt{\log n}$? \sref{2208}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Improve the pinned-distance exponent to n\textasciicircum{}(1-o(1)) or the grid-scale bound. \sref{2208}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Must some point of every large planar configuration see nearly as many different distances as there are points, at the proposed sharp scale?
