\section*{Human Contribution: Scott Duke Kominers}

\subsection*{Multi-covering extension of OpenAI Result 092}

\paragraph{Paper and attribution.}
Scott Duke Kominers, \emph{Multi-coverings by lattice translates of convex bodies},
\href{https://hexagonmath.org/2610.00165v1}{hexagon:2610.00165v1},
is a preliminary manuscript dated October 8, 2026.
It develops the single-lattice construction in OpenAI's Result~092 into a
bound for arbitrary covering multiplicity. The author credits substantial
LLM assistance, principally GPT-6 Astra, while taking responsibility for
the problem, methods, exposition, and any errors. This record is a summary
of that authored contribution.

\paragraph{Lattice multi-coverings (Theorem 1.1).}
For a convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ and a full-rank lattice $\Lambda$,
write
\[
 N_{\Lambda,K}(x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbf 1_K(x-\lambda),
 \qquad
 \rho=\frac{\operatorname{vol}_n(K)}{\det\Lambda}.
\]
A lattice $k$-fold covering requires $N_{\Lambda,K}(x)\geq k$ at every
$x$, with one translate for each distinct lattice point. Its density
$\rho$ is the mean multiplicity. Let $\theta_{\mathrm{lat},k}(K)$
be the infimum of these densities. Theorem~1.1 states that, for every
$\varepsilon>0$, a constant $C_\varepsilon$ depending only on
$\varepsilon$ gives
\[
 \theta_{\mathrm{lat},k}(K)
 \leq \max\{(1+\varepsilon)k,\ C_\varepsilon n\log n\}
 \quad(n\geq2,\ k\geq1).
\]
No symmetry assumption is imposed on $K$. In particular, the density
approaches the unavoidable lower bound $k$ uniformly over bodies and
dimensions when $k/(n\log n)$ tends to infinity.

\paragraph{Prescribed density and simultaneous coverings (Theorem 1.2).}
For one body and $0<\varepsilon<1$, every prescribed density
$\rho\geq A_\varepsilon n\log n$ can be realized by a lattice satisfying
$N_{\Lambda,K}(x)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho$ everywhere, in every
dimension $n\geq2$. In sufficiently large dimension, the theorem also
constructs one lattice of a prescribed covolume $\Delta$ for up to
$\exp(n^{c_0})$ bodies, for an absolute $c_0>0$. This simultaneous
statement assumes that their uniform distributions share a covariance
matrix and that each mean
$\rho_j=\operatorname{vol}_n(K_j)/\Delta$ exceeds the threshold.
The bodies can have different volumes; their means need not agree.
There is no matching pointwise upper bound on multiplicity or
claim about a typical random lattice.

\paragraph{Worst-case order (Corollary 1.3).}
Combining Theorem~1.1 with Li--Liu's earlier one-fold lattice lower bound,
the paper obtains worst-case density of order $k+n\log n$, both over all
convex bodies and over origin-symmetric convex bodies. This is a
worst-case lattice statement; it does not identify the optimal
one-fold translative density for a particular body such as a ball.

\paragraph{Outline of the argument.}
The proof adapts OpenAI's horizontal--vertical decomposition of a lattice.
Approximately Gaussian section volumes provide layer weights, and
averaging a bounded terminal block over its cosets retains almost all of
the mean while keeping individual selected weights small. Independent
spatial thinning turns a mean-hole estimate into a Laplace-transform
bound for section counts. A random shear combines those estimates to
make the region of insufficient multiplicity very small. A small
dilation eliminates that region; a final lattice rescaling gives the
prescribed mean. Equal covariance permits a common normalization in the
simultaneous version.

\paragraph{Scope relative to this problem.}
The recorded problem asks for the optimal asymptotic density of a
one-fold covering by congruent Euclidean balls. Specializing
Theorem~1.1 to a ball gives a lattice $k$-fold covering with the stated
density bound and, at $k=1$, an $O(n\log n)$ upper bound.
For multiplicities large compared with $n\log n$, it gives density
asymptotic to $k$. These conclusions are partial progress relative to
the recorded one-fold question: they do not determine its optimal
asymptotic density. Corollary~1.3 concerns worst-case convex bodies
under the lattice restriction and supplies no matching one-fold lower
bound for balls.
