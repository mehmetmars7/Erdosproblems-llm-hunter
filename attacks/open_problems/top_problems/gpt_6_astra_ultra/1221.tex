\section{Bounded Generalized Star Height}\label{1221}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 1221 · Algebra}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



Generalized regular expressions over a finite alphabet allow union, concatenation, Kleene star and complement relative to all finite words. Star height is the maximum number of nested stars; Boolean operations do not themselves increase it. For a regular language $L$, minimize this height over all generalized expressions defining $L$. Ask whether one finite bound works for every regular language. \sref{1221}{1} \sref{1221}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Can all regular languages be expressed with generalized regular expressions of bounded star height? \sref{1221}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Can every regular language be described using a universally bounded nesting depth of repetition, once complements are allowed?

\subsection{Research attempt: the exact unary case and the limits of state elimination}
\textbf{Status.} The universal-alphabet boundedness question remains unresolved here. We prove that every unary regular language has generalized star height at most one and that this bound is attained. The inspected thesis [S2] treats the generalized problem; its terminology includes complement, unlike ordinary star height.

\subsubsection{1. Following a unary automaton to its eventual cycle}
Let $L\subseteq\{a\}^*$ be accepted by a deterministic automaton with $q$ states. Write $s_n$ for the state reached after $a^n$. Among $s_0,\ldots,s_q$ two agree, say $s_\mu=s_{\mu+p}$ with $0\leq\mu<\mu+p\leq q$. Determinism implies $s_{n+p}=s_n$ for all $n\geq\mu$. Let $E$ be the finite accepted lengths below $\mu$, and let
\[R=\{r:0\leq r<p,\ s_{\mu+r}\text{ is accepting}\}.\]
Then the exact expression
\[L=\bigcup_{n\in E}\{a^n\}\ \cup\
\bigcup_{r\in R} a^{\mu+r}(a^p)^*\tag{1}\]
has no nested stars. In (1), powers denote literal finite concatenations, not iteration operators. Empty $E$ or $R$ give empty unions. The construction is effective with $\mu+p\leq q$ and uses no complementation.

\subsubsection{2. Why height zero cannot do the same job}
Every star-free expression over a unary alphabet defines a finite or cofinite set of words. Prove this by structural induction. The atomic languages are finite. Union and complement preserve the family of finite/cofinite sets. For concatenation identify words with their lengths in $\mathbb N_0$, so concatenation is sumset. Two finite sets have finite sumset. If one factor is empty the sumset is empty. A nonempty finite set plus a cofinite set is cofinite, since one translate already contains all sufficiently large integers. Two cofinite sets also have cofinite sumset: fix one element in one factor and translate the other. This verifies the induction for every operation allowed at height zero.

Conversely a finite language is a finite union of literal words, and a cofinite one is the complement of such a union. Hence unary generalized height zero is exactly finite/cofinite. The language $\{a^{2n}:n\geq0\}$ is neither finite nor cofinite, so its generalized height is exactly one. The bound in the unary theorem is sharp, not merely an artifact of (1).

\subsubsection{3. Why an automaton-size bound is not a universal constant}
For a general finite alphabet, state elimination represents paths using recurrence
\[R^{(k)}_{ij}=R^{(k-1)}_{ij}\ \cup\
R^{(k-1)}_{ik}(R^{(k-1)}_{kk})^*R^{(k-1)}_{kj},\]
where paths at stage $k$ may use only the first $k$ states internally. This gives a finite expression, but the newly introduced star may contain earlier stars. The recurrence therefore supplies a bound growing with the number of eliminated states, not a constant independent of the input automaton. Having complement available does not automatically flatten that nesting; an identity doing so must be proved.

\subsubsection{4. Checks and residual obstruction}
The diagnostic enumerates all unary deterministic automata with at most four states, initial state 0 and every accepting subset. It compares direct acceptance with the periodic representation (1) for lengths through $4q+10$. The proof, rather than that finite test, establishes equality at all lengths. The height-zero argument is deliberately unary: over two letters there are infinite noncofinite star-free languages, for instance all words containing the letter $a$, expressible using the complement of the empty language and concatenation. Thus one cannot extend the finite/cofinite characterization to larger alphabets. A universal bound on generalized height for arbitrary alphabets and arbitrary transition structures is the exact missing conclusion.
