\section{The Least Prime Missing a Consecutive Product}\label{2240}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 2240 · Number Theory}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,

$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.

Define $Q_{n,k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n+j)$ and $q(n,k)=\min\{p:p\text{ prime},\ p\nmid Q_{n,k}\}$. The latter exists since only finitely many primes divide $Q_{n,k}$. For fixed $k$, the proposed upper bound is equivalently $\limsup_{n\to\infty}q(n,k)/\log n\leq1$. \sref{2240}{1} \sref{2240}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Let $k\geq 2$ and $q(n,k)$ denote the least prime which does not divide $\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}(n+i)$. Is it true that, if $k$ is fixed and $n$ is sufficiently large, we have $ q(n,k)<(1+o(1))\log n? $ \sref{2240}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

For a fixed number of consecutive factors, is the smallest prime absent from their product asymptotically bounded by the logarithm of their location?
