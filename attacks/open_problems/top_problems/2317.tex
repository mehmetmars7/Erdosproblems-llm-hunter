\section{Uniform Growth between Successive Diagonal Ramsey Numbers}\label{2317}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 2317 · Combinatorics}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.

A graph has a vertex set $V$ and edges that are unordered pairs of distinct vertices. A subgraph need not be induced unless that word is stated. A proper vertex colouring gives adjacent vertices different colours; $\chi(G)$ is the least number of colours.

$R(G,H)$ is the least order of a complete graph whose every red--blue edge colouring has a red copy of $G$ or a blue copy of $H$. For integer arguments, $R(s,t)=R(K_s,K_t)$. Write $R(G)=R(G,G)$ and $R(k)=R(K_k,K_k)$. The notation $R(G;q)$ instead uses $q$ colours and the same target in each colour. \sref{2317}{1} \sref{2317}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Is it true that $ \frac{R(n+1)}{R(n)}\geq 1+c $ for some constant $c>0$, for all large $n$? Is it true that $ R(n+1)-R(n) \gg n^2? $ \sref{2317}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Are neighbouring diagonal Ramsey numbers separated by a fixed multiplicative factor, and by at least a quadratic additive amount?
