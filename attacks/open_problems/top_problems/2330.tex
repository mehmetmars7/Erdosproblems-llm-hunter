\section{Many Circumradii in General-Position Point Sets}\label{2330}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 2330 · Geometry}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural

Every triple has a unique circumcircle because collinear triples are excluded. Count distinct radius values, not circles themselves. The no-four-concyclic condition is an additional restriction on the point configuration.

Distances are Euclidean. Distinct distances mean distinct numerical values; when a distance is said to occur a certain number of times, unordered pairs of distinct points are counted. Congruence allows any Euclidean isometry, including reflection. \sref{2330}{1} \sref{2330}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Let $h(n)$ be maximal such that in any $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (with no three on a line and no four on a circle) there are at least $h(n)$ many circles of different radii passing through three points. Estimate $h(n)$. \sref{2330}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

How many different circumcircle radii must arise from triples of a planar set having no collinear triples or concyclic quadruples?
