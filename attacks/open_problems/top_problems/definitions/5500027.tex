\section*{Hexahedral Meshing}\label{5500027}
{\small UnsolvedMath ID 5500027 · AMR-054-0027 · Geometry · AMR Open Problem Lists}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
Let $P\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a polyhedral solid homeomorphic to a closed
three-ball. This makes the connected-boundary domain convention explicit;
simply connected solids with separate cavity boundaries would enlarge
the class. The boundary is given a conforming quadrilateral mesh: faces
are planar quadrilaterals with disjoint relative interiors, meet
edge-to-edge, and cover $\partial P$. Assume the number $b$ of boundary
quadrilaterals is even.

A geometric hexahedral mesh is a finite face-to-face subdivision of
$P$ into embedded polyhedral cells combinatorially equivalent to a
cube. Every cell has six planar quadrilateral faces; cells need not
be congruent, rectangular, or aligned with coordinate axes. New
interior vertices are allowed, but the induced boundary mesh must be
exactly the prescribed one, without subdividing its faces.

Does every such boundary mesh extend to a geometric hexahedral mesh?
The parity condition is necessary: if $h$ is the number of cells and
$i$ the number of interior faces, then $6h=2i+b$.

The source reports extensions by topological cells and by cells with
straight edges but possibly nonplanar faces. The unresolved formulation
retained here requires planar faces. Consequently, a topological
extension alone does not answer it, and ``hexahedron'' does not mean
an axis-aligned rectangular box.
\subsection{Short English statement}
Can every even quadrilateral mesh on the boundary of a polyhedral ball
be filled by cube-shaped combinatorial cells whose faces are all planar?
