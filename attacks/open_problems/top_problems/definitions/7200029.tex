\section{Reinhardt's conjecture}\label{7200029}
{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 7200029 · Geometry}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.


A centrally symmetric convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^2$ is compact, convex, has nonempty interior, and is invariant under reflection in some point. A full-rank lattice $\Lambda$ gives a packing if the translates $K+v$, $v\in\Lambda$, have disjoint interiors. Its density is $|K|/\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)$, where the covolume is the area of a fundamental parallelogram. Let $\delta(K)$ be the maximum of these lattice-packing densities. For this class of planar bodies it also equals the best unrestricted packing density. Reinhardt's smoothed octagon is obtained by clipping the corners of a regular octagon by hyperbolic arcs, meeting the remaining sides with continuous tangents; each arc's asymptotes extend two sides of the original octagon. Its density is
\[c_R=\frac{8-\sqrt{32}-\log 2}{\sqrt8-1}.\]
The conjecture includes uniqueness up to invertible affine transformations.
\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Does every centrally symmetric convex planar body satisfy $\delta(K)\geq c_R$, with equality precisely for affine images of Reinhardt's smoothed octagon? \sref{7200029}{2}
\subsection{Short English statement}
Is Reinhardt’s octagon with hyperbolically rounded corners the unique shape, up to affine changes, whose densest packing has the smallest density among centrally symmetric convex planar bodies?
