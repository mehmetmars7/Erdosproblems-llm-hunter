\section*{Monochromatic Triangles}\label{5500058}
{\small UnsolvedMath ID 5500058 · AMR-054-0058 · Combinatorics · AMR Open Problem Lists}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
Let $T=\{a,b,c\}\subset\mathbb R^2$ be the three distinct,
noncollinear vertices of a prescribed triangle. A three-coloring
of the plane is a map $\chi:\mathbb R^2\to\{1,2,3\}$, with no
measurability or continuity assumption. A congruent copy of $T$
is $g(T)$ for a Euclidean isometry $g$; translations, rotations,
and reflections are allowed, but scaling is not.

For every triangle $T$, does there exist such a coloring with
\[
             \{\chi(g(a)),\chi(g(b)),\chi(g(c))\}
                     \text{ having at least two elements}
                 \quad\text{for every isometry }g\,?
\]
Equivalently, can one partition the plane into three color
classes so that no class contains a congruent copy of this
particular triangle?

The coloring may depend on the triangle, including its side
lengths. The problem does not ask for one coloring that avoids
all triangle shapes simultaneously. Only the colors of the
three vertices matter: colors along the edges or in the
interior of their convex hull play no role. Using at most three
colors is permitted. Replacing congruence by similarity, imposing
regularity on the color classes, or requiring a single coloring
for every $T$ would produce stronger and different questions.
\subsection{Short English statement}
For each prescribed triangle, can the plane be colored with three colors so that no congruent copy has all three vertices the same color?
