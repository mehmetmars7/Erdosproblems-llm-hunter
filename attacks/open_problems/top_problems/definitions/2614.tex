\section*{Kourovka Notebook Problem 21.105}\label{2614}
{\small UnsolvedMath ID 2614 · KOU-21.105 · Group Theory · Kourovka Notebook - New Problems, Issue 21}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
A group word in $r$ variables is an element $w\in F_r$, the free group on symbols $x_1,\ldots,x_r$. Substituting $g_1,\ldots,g_r$ in a group $G$ gives an element $w(g_1,\ldots,g_r)$. Define the set of word values and the verbal subgroup by
\[
 G_w=\{w(g_1,\ldots,g_r):g_i\in G\},\qquad
 w(G)=\langle G_w\rangle.
\]
The subgroup brackets mean all finite products of elements of $G_w$ and their inverses.

A group is residually finite if for every nonidentity element $g$ there is a homomorphism $\theta:G\to Q$ to a finite group such that $\theta(g)\ne1$. Equivalently, the intersection of all finite-index normal subgroups of $G$ is trivial. The group itself may be infinite and need not be finitely generated.

Is every word concise in this class? In full quantifiers, for every integer $r\ge1$, every word $w\in F_r$, and every residually finite group $G$, does
\[
 |G_w|<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |w(G)|<\infty
\]
hold? Finiteness of the value set is the hypothesis, whereas finiteness of its generated subgroup is the desired conclusion. A finite set of arbitrary group elements can generate an infinite subgroup, so their origin as all values of one fixed word matters. No universal numerical bound on $|w(G)|$ is requested here.
\subsection{Short English statement}
In any residually finite group, must finitely many values of a fixed group word generate a finite subgroup?
