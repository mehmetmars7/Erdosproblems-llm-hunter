\section*{Geometry of Curves and Surfaces — Problem 1.1}\label{7000001}
{\small UnsolvedMath ID 7000001 · AMR-069-0001 · Geometry · AMR Open Problem Lists}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
Let $M$ be a compact connected smooth two-manifold without boundary,
and let $f_0:M\to\mathbb R^3$ be a $C^2$ embedding. Its first
fundamental form is the metric
$g_u(v,w)=\langle df_0(v),df_0(w)\rangle$ on tangent vectors at $u$.
A $C^2$ isometric flex is a continuous family of $C^2$ embeddings
$f_t:M\to\mathbb R^3$, $0\le t\le1$, with
\[
       \langle df_t(v),df_t(w)\rangle=g_u(v,w)
       \quad\text{for every }u,v,w,t.
\]
Continuity is taken in the $C^2$ topology. Thus intrinsic lengths
are unchanged at every stage, not just at the endpoints.

Does there exist such a family for which at least one image
$f_t(M)$ is not congruent to $f_0(M)$ by a Euclidean rigid motion?
The initial surface may be nonconvex and of any allowable genus.
Every intermediate surface must remain embedded and $C^2$.

Two isolated isometric but noncongruent surfaces do not provide a
flex: a continuous path joining them is required. Likewise,
flexible polyhedra or $C^1$ constructions do not meet the $C^2$
regularity requirement. ``Closed'' means compact with no boundary,
so bending a sheet with a free edge is outside the question.
\subsection{Short English statement}
Can a closed twice-continuously-differentiable surface in ordinary
three-dimensional space bend continuously into a different shape
without stretching or self-intersection?
