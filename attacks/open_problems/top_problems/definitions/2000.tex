\section{A Continuous Limiting Distribution for Prime Gaps}\label{2000}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 2000 · Number Theory}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



Put $g_n=(p_{n+1}-p_n)/\log n$ for $n\geq2$. The required function is $f(c)=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\#\{2\leq n\leq N:g_n<c\}$. The one undefined term at $n=1$ is omitted and has no effect on density.

$p_j$ denotes the $j$th positive prime in increasing order, starting with $p_1=2$. The prime-counting function is $\pi(x)=\#\{p\leq x:p\text{ is prime}\}$. Consecutive primes are consecutive in this full sequence. \sref{2000}{1} \sref{2000}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
For every $c\geq 0$ the density $f(c)$ of integers for which $ \frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log n}< c $ exists and is a continuous function of $c$. \sref{2000}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Do consecutive prime gaps, divided by the logarithm of their indices, have a continuous limiting distribution?
