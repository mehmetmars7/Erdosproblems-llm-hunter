\section{Unbounded Numbers of New Prime Factors in Consecutive Integers}\label{2368}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 2368 · Number Theory}

Prime factors are counted distinctly. A prime dividing $n+k$ occurred among the preceding $k$ integers exactly when it is at most $k$. Hence $v(n,k)=\#\{p:p\mid n+k,\ p>k\}$, with no preceding integers when $k=0$. \sref{2368}{1} \sref{2368}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
For $k\geq 0$ and $n\geq 1$ let $v(n,k)$ count the prime factors of $n+k$ which do not divide $n+i$ for $0\leq i<k$. Equivalently, $v(n,k)$ counts the number of prime factors of $n+k$ which are $>k$.
Is it true that $ v_0(n)=\max_{k\geq 0}v(n,k)\to \infty $ as $n\to \infty$? \sref{2368}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Prove v\_0(n) tends to infinity. \sref{2368}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Starting from larger integers, must some later term introduce arbitrarily many prime factors absent from every preceding term of that block?
