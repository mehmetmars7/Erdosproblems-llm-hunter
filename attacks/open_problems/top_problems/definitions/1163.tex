\section{The Inscribed Square Problem}\label{1163}
{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 1163 · Geometry}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.


A Jordan curve in the plane is the image \(\Gamma=\gamma(S^1)\)
of a continuous injective map \(\gamma:S^1\to\mathbb R^2\), where
\(S^1=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=1\}\).
Let \(R(x,y)=(-y,x)\) denote rotation through a right angle.
An inscribed square means four distinct points of \(\Gamma\) that
are the vertices of a nondegenerate Euclidean square; its sides
need not lie on the curve.
\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
For every Jordan curve \(\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb R^2\), are there
\(c\in\mathbb R^2\) and \(u\in\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\) such that
\[
 c+u,\quad c+Ru,\quad c-u,\quad c-Ru
       \quad\text{all belong to }\Gamma?
\]
No smoothness, rectifiability, Lipschitz or convexity hypothesis
is imposed on the Jordan curve. \sref{1163}{2}
\subsection{Short English statement}
Must every simple closed continuous plane curve contain the four vertices of a square of positive side length?
