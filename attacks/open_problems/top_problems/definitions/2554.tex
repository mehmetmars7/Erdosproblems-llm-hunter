\section*{Kourovka Notebook Problem 21.45}\label{2554}
{\small UnsolvedMath ID 2554 · KOU-21.45 · Group Theory · Kourovka Notebook - New Problems, Issue 21}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
A finite presentation of a group is an expression
\[
 G=\langle x_1,\ldots,x_m\mid r_1,\ldots,r_s\rangle
   =F_m/\langle\!\langle r_1,\ldots,r_s\rangle\!\rangle
\]
with $m,s$ finite, where $F_m$ is free on the listed generators and
the denominator is the normal subgroup generated by the relators.
The group is simple if it is nontrivial and has no proper nontrivial
normal subgroup.
For a finitely generated group, let $d(G)$ be the smallest cardinality
of a generating set, allowing arbitrary elements of $G$ as generators.

Does there exist an infinite simple group $G$ with a finite presentation
and $d(G)>2$? Equivalently, one seeks a finitely presented infinite simple
group such that
\[
 \langle a,b\rangle\ne G\qquad\text{for every }a,b\in G.
\]
The subgroup $\langle a,b\rangle$ includes products using inverses and
may itself be infinite; it must simply be proper in every case.

The number of generators displayed in a chosen presentation is not
necessarily minimal. Presenting a group with three or more named
generators therefore does not prove the desired lower bound. A witness
must rule out every alternative pair, while also proving infinitude,
simplicity, and finite presentability. The question asks for some finite
minimal generator number at least three; no particular number is prescribed.
Infinite simple groups lacking finite presentations do not settle the
problem even if they require more than two generators.
\subsection{Short English statement}
Does some finitely presented infinite simple group require at least three generators?
