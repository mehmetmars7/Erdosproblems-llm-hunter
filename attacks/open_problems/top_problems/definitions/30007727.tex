\section*{Drug pricing and valuable innovation}\label{30007727}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
Consider pharmaceutical firms developing treatments for a prespecified set of diseases in the United States over fifteen years. Let \(p\in\{0,1\}\) represent a clearly specified drug-price regulation versus the existing reimbursement and patent regime. For candidate treatment \(j\), let \(A_j(p)\in\{0,1\}\) indicate approval within the horizon, \(N_j(p)\) discounted treated patients, \(b_j(p)\) independently assessed incremental health benefit per patient, and \(R_j(p)\) real development and delivery cost. Define the innovation-value contrast
\[\Delta V=E\!\left[\sum_j\{\lambda A_j(1)N_j(1)b_j(1)-R_j(1)\}-\sum_j\{\lambda A_j(0)N_j(0)b_j(0)-R_j(0)\}\right],\]
where \(\lambda>0\) is a stated monetary health valuation. Index a common finite universe of potential candidates across policies, including entry and abandonment; define the patient-benefit term as zero for unapproved candidates and retain incurred development costs. Require integrable costs and benefits. Identify or bound the effect on clinically valuable innovation rather than equate patent or approval counts with welfare. Explain how expected prices, financing, indication selection, and patient access enter firm decisions. Credible policy variation must separate regulation from changing science and disease demand. Report effects by unmet need and health-benefit distribution. The cited review supports relevant mechanisms, but does not verify this precise fifteen-year net-value question as an explicit open statement.
